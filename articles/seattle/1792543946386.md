Following the explosive growth of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) in
2016, a debate has emerged over the nature of the DSA and how to relate to it
on the left. Although it is clear that the DSA is a reformist organization, its
size and popularity among newly radicalizing socialists makes it an important
phenomenon to address on the left. Among revolutionaries, there is broad
agreement that the DSA’s politics, at least as they are today, are a dead end.
The question is, how do we get the DSA, or the DSA’s members, to see that as
well?

Even with a decade of attempts by various groups to shape the DSA’s politics
and priorities, the terms of the debate over how to intervene remain relatively
fixed. There are those who argue for joining the DSA and trying to steer the
organization from the inside towards the left. There are others who argue for a
“french-turn” strategy, where revolutionaries join the DSA for a period of
time, recruit, and ultimately break away again, hopefully with more members
than they had before. Finally, there are those who argue for remaining outside
of the DSA and working in coalition with DSA where possible. This piece is a
contribution towards this debate.

### What is the DSA?

The DSA was formed in 1982, through the merger of two prior organizations, the
Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee (DSOC) and the New American Movement
(NAM). The newly formed DSA inherited the strategic orientation of pushing the
Democratic Party to the left – typically referred to as the “realignment”
strategy – from the DSOC. The DSA’s intellectual heritage comes primarily from
the reformist socialist Michael Harrington, who championed the realignment
strategy for most of his life.

The DSA was a lifeless organization with only a few thousand paper members, and
even fewer active ones, for most of its existence. Its fortunes changed in 2016
following Bernie Sanders’ first presidential campaign after Sanders referred to
himself as a “democratic socialist,” leading to an explosive growth of
membership. Today, it is the largest socialist organization in the US, with
over 120,000 paper members.

The preponderance of paper members makes it difficult to generalize about the
political character of the DSA’s membership. Anarchists, Stalinists,
social-democrats, liberal reformists, and everything else in between can find a
home in the DSA. However, its leadership is resolutely reformist. While there
have been political challenges within the DSA from the left, the leadership has
consistently defeated them; the canonical example of this was the 2021 Bowman
affair, where DSA’s Palestine Working Group attempted to have the DSA’s elected
politician Jamaal Bowman censured for supporting the Israeli occupation, and
[found themselves
censured](https://tempestmag.org/2025/01/dsa-never-recovered-from-bowman/)
instead. While Bowman’s support of the Israeli occupation is bad enough, the
affair revealed something more fundamentally damning about the nature of DSA:
its elected members are not accountable to the DSA’s membership, and moreover,
the DSA’s leadership works hard keep it that way.

While it is true that the DSA has moved left on some of its original positions,
such as unapologetic support for the state of Israel ([much to the chagrin of
Harringtonites like Maurice
Isserman](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2024/06/what-failure-american-communism-should-teach-left/678697/)),
its reformist tradition remains largely intact. As Doug Greene notes in his
biography of Michael Harrington entitled A Failure of Vision, the DSA’s most
prominent intellectuals such as Bhaskar Sunkara and Seth Ackerman explicitly
acknowledge Harrington as an influence on their thinking, however much they
disagree with some of his more embarrassing positions today.

Although the DSA’s strategic orientation has received various re-brands, such
as the “party surrogate model” and the “dirty break,” ultimately these
strategies boil down to the [old strategy of
realignment](https://redspark.red/perspectives/national-mamdani-the-dsa-and-the-reactionary-gravity-of-the-democrats/).
In practice, this means that the DSA supports nominally socialist candidates in
the Democratic Party with the aim of transforming it into a social-democratic
party. Structurally, this makes the DSA the far- left flank of the Democratic
Party.

The DSA’s size and preeminence in the US socialist movement means that all
socialists, regardless of their relationship (or lack thereof) are required to
have a stance on it. The next sections will cover what policies have so far
been tried and evaluate their effectiveness.

### The trap door of joining the DSA

Many individual socialists and organizations have joined the DSA in an effort
to change its politics. One decent example of this is the Reform & Revolution
(R&R) caucus, which originally began as a split from Socialist Alternative.
While Socialist Alternative formally maintained opposition to the Democratic
Party, upon joining the DSA in 2019, R&R’s stance switched towards supporting
the “dirty break,” a strategy which entails running candidates in the
Democratic Party for now, with the aim of breaking with the Democratic Party at
some point in the future.

The trajectory of R&R’s stance on the Democratic Party is quite instructive. In
2021, R&R called for a [“principled” application of the dirty
break](https://reformandrevolution.org/2021/01/21/winning-the-dirty-break-under-biden-what-next-for-dsa/),
warning that the dirty break could become nothing more than the “opportunist
practice of adapting to the Democratic Party.” By 2023, the trajectory of this
strategy had become clear to R&R, and they
[bemoaned](https://reformandrevolution.org/2023/05/20/online-panel-discussion-5-20-a-vision-for-dsa-2023-%E2%86%92-2028-on-the-road-to-socialism-beyond-the-left-wing-of-the-possible/)
how the dirty break had become the “dirty stay.” Finally, R&R has come around
to at least [partial
acceptance](https://reformandrevolution.org/2025/09/25/contradictions-of-the-party-surrogate-model/)
of the DSA’s “party surrogate” model, which argues for running candidates on
the Democratic Party ballot line but remaining as “independent” as possible
from the Democrats otherwise. How much longer will it be before they are just
outright arguing for “realigning” the Democratic Party?

Individual socialists fare much worse than those who join as groups. Even an
educated, principled revolutionary will find it difficult to avoid adapting to
reformism. Without even a small group of fellow revolutionaries, the DSA’s
reformism becomes nearly impossible to avoid.

If we just recall the conclusion of our previous analysis – that the DSA
effectively functions as the left- wing of the Democratic Party – this process
should be unsurprising. It is the same process which has plagued the left since
the New Deal. Socialists and progressive activists, seeking to “achieve
something” or to be “pragmatic” enter the orbit of the Democratic Party hoping
to transform it, only to find themselves transformed instead. Their presence as
loyal critics rather than principled opponents sows illusions among the
oppressed in the world’s oldest capitalist party, rather than exposing it for
the enemy that it really is.

With regards to the DSA, when revolutionaries join it, they give speeches at
rallies, promote the organization and create the impression that revolutionary
politics has a home in the DSA. But the reality is that when the rubber hits
the road, reformism runs the show. There may be talk occasionally of breaking
from the Democrats, but much like cold fusion, it is always years away.

### A “French Turn”?

Some revolutionaries argue for a strategy of joining the DSA, not to reform it
from within, but purely to grow in membership and expose the leadership. The
idea of this strategy, called the “French Turn” after its usage by French
Trotskyists during the 1930s, is to expose the DSA’s leadership, and break from
the DSA shortly afterwards, hopefully with a stronger, larger yet still
principled revolutionary organization.

In the 1930s, this strategy [was
undertaken](https://www.bolshevik.org/1917/no9/no09frnt.pdf) by the Trotskyist
International Communist League (ICL). The ICL was formed in 1933 after the
complete Stalinization of the Comintern (communist international) became clear.
In the initial period of the ICL’s existence, its members were present in
several countries alongside mass reformist workers’ parties. In 1934, Trotsky
argued for entry into these parties to get more opportunities to expose the
leadership and grow in membership. He argued that the ICL would be doomed to
irrelevance if it ignored these mass workers’ parties.

The tactic was hotly debated and more than a few members of the ICL left in
protest of the tactic, arguing that entry into a reformist organization was
opportunism. Yet ultimately, the tactic proved successful and correct for the
time period in which it was applied. The French section of the ICL managed to
triple in size. The American section doubled and would go on to found the
Socialist Workers’ Party (SWP).

While this strategy was correct in the 1930s, the circumstances today are such
that applying this strategy to the DSA would be a grave mistake. In general,
this strategy requires specific objective and subjective factors to be present,
otherwise failure is nearly guaranteed. Even if those factors are present, the
“french turn” strategy should be regarded as quite risky in comparison to the
alternative of remaining organizationally distinct.

The first main objective factor is the level of radicalization. The leadership
of the DSA would have to be widely discredited among the membership.
Furthermore, the overall political environment would have to be shifting
sharply to the left, at least among the working class. Reformism would have to
be facing a fundamental challenge among the working class for the “french turn”
strategy to work. This was the case in the 1930s; it is difficult to make the
case that such a radicalization is widespread enough in the working class
today, much less the DSA.

The second objective factor is that the organization to enter would have to be
a so-called “bourgeois workers’ party,” or an organization with a working-class
social basis but a bourgeois program. This was the type of organization that
Lenin had in mind when he famously argued that the British Communists affiliate
with the Labor Party. The issue is that the DSA is attached to the Democratic
Party; the basis of its organization is electing “progressive” bourgeois
politicians. In France, the Trotskyists were forced to leave over the
reformists adopting this very policy – and the DSA is not just already there,
but in fact, is entirely dependent on tailing the Democrats for its very
existence.

As for the subjective factors, i.e. revolutionary organization, revolutionaries
would have to already be considerable in size and have very clear, measurable
objectives to be achieved over a relatively short time horizon. Furthermore,
they would have to launch into fierce critique of the leadership of the DSA and
indeed, the DSA as a whole from the moment they step foot within it. Needless
to say, it would be difficult to last very long within the DSA with this
posture.

It is also debatable whether an international is necessary; in the case of the
French Communists, even the short period of a year had led many to adapt to the
reformist SFIO, and they were reluctant to leave when the question of the
popular front arose. It took pressure from Trotsky and the international, as
well as the objective circumstances, to help the French Trotskyists to draw the
correct conclusions. Lacking this, it is very possible that we lose the forest
for the trees and let the intervention go on for an indefinite period of time.

We can say with confidence that neither the objective nor the subjective
factors are present anywhere in the US. While the DSA’s reputation has been
damaged over time due to its reformism and attachment to the Democratic Party,
reformism itself remains highly popular on the left and in the working class.
The DSA’s membership is not at odds with its leadership over questions of
fundamental strategy. As for the subjective factors, we can confidently say
that there is no organization capable of pulling off a maneuver such as this
today, including our own. At the bare minimum, such an organization’s
membership would have to be numbered in the thousands, not tens or hundreds,
and would have to be comprised solely of principled revolutionaries.

### Can the DSA be convinced to make the break?

Right now, it is quite obvious that the DSA is not imminently about to break
with the Democratic Party. However, it is worth going through the thought
exercise to understand what it would mean for the DSA to break with the
Democrats, as this informs our analysis of whether it is possible at all.

A useful piece of evidence to consider is a relatively recent
[debate](https://www.leftvoice.org/its-time-for-a-decisive-step-toward-a-socialist-working-class-party-dsa-must-break-with-the-democratic-party/)
that Left Voice engaged in with the DSA’s leadership. In an editorial written
on November 20th, 2025, Left Voice published an editorial arguing that the DSA
needs to break with the Democrats as a necessary step towards a truly
independent socialist party.

In response, a prominent and prolific writer who goes by the pen name JoeWrote
[defended](https://www.joewrote.com/p/dsas-electoral-strategy-works-a-response)
the DSA’s electoral strategy, citing their (electoral) results and making the
typical reformist arguments. Suffice to say that JoeWrote’s defense would not
convince any left-wing critics of the DSA. To give an idea of the article’s
arguments, it argues that the DSA does not anchor itself solely to the
Democratic Party, because it also supports candidates in the Working Families
Party (WFP). For context, the WFP is an organization which can only honestly be
described as a satellite of the Democratic Party; if we were to accept that WFP
candidates aren’t Democrats, we would also have to accept the absurd conclusion
that Bernie Sanders isn’t a Democrat either, since he lists his party as
“independent.”

It is not the purpose of this article to litigate who won the debate, but
rather to question its terms and if it should have happened at all. Engaging in
debate with others on the left only really makes sense if our goals are broadly
shared. If they are different, then the goal shifts from simple debates to
arguing that the goal itself is wrong – and disagreements of this kind are
usually only clarified in struggle. Although JoeWrote’s piece is mostly a mix
of dishonesty and tired reformist arguments, it does have a rather clarifying
and biting critique against Left Voice’s editorial:

> The editors’ incorrect accusation that DSA is betraying our membership is
> indicative of the long-standing ultra-left, Trotskyist tendency to mistake
> *their* [emphasis original] criticism of a socialist organization (in this case,
> DSA) as widely shared throughout that organization, despite all evidence to the
> contrary. […] If “tens of thousands” of DSA members felt betrayed, they would
> say it and leave the organization.

Despite the inflammatory language, JoeWrote is actually identifying a
fundamental flaw with Left Voice’s critique: most members of the DSA actually
are reformists who are satisfied with the DSA’s core social-democratic
politics. They would not be satisfied if the DSA’s leadership suddenly
undertook actions contrary to these core politics. Although members of the DSA
will often find themselves disappointed, they cannot honestly say they feel
*betrayed*. The DSA’s reputation as an electoral, reformist organization attached
to the Democrats is very well established. There is, from what we can tell, no
large contingency of members in the DSA trying to force the question of
breaking from the Democrats today. At most, they argue for an eventual break
that always seems to remain too far in the future to have any practical
significance.

At some point, we must assume that the DSA’s strategy is working, but that its
actual goals are simply different from the goals stated in the DSA’s public
propaganda. If we make this assumption, we must conclude that the DSA’s actual
goal is to become a conduit for progressive activists to entrench themselves as
elected officials in the Democratic Party, not to fundamentally transform
society. If we take this as their goal, we can see that the DSA’s strategy is
working quite well actually!

All that said, let’s suspend our disbelief for a moment and say that the DSA
did break with the Democrats. What would happen?

First, there would be sharp, existential conflicts between the leadership and
membership. If members overwhelmingly wanted to break with the Democrats
against the leaders’ wishes, the most likely result would be splits and members
leaving en mass. If the leadership wanted to break against the membership’s
wishes, they would be quickly deposed and replaced with leaders who reflect the
reformist politics of the membership. Even if a well-organized educational
campaign were undertaken within the DSA to convince the membership, the
preponderance of paper members means that it would be nearly impossible to
accurately gauge the potential reaction of the membership to such a change;
most likely, they would reject the changes and drop their membership.

Perhaps worse for the DSA would be the effect of breaking with the Democrats on
its relationship with the elected politicians under its banner. Given that the
DSA’s elected politicians are accountable to the Democratic Party electoral
machine and donor network, rather than to the DSA, almost all of them would
choose to lose their membership in the DSA over their status as Democrats. This
would essentially eliminate the entire appeal of the DSA by destroying its
claim to being the “practical” option for socialists seeking to change the
world.

Even if all of these obstacles could be overcome and the organization could
come through the process of breaking relatively intact, it would remain a
fundamentally reformist organization. Even if organized along class lines, its
status would at best be like that of the historical British Labor Party.
Following Vladimir Lenin’s analysis in *Left-Wing Communism: An Infantile
Disorder*, revolutionaries would be obligated to support it critically and for
the sole purpose of exposing its leaders to the working class. Independent
revolutionary organization would remain essential!

### Why working *with* the DSA beats working *within* it

Our policy towards the DSA is based on the principle of revolutionary honesty.
We believe that the DSA’s goals are fundamentally distinct from ours, and we
make no secret about that. We do not “advise” or try to convince the DSA as a
whole that it should break from the Democrats for the same reason we don’t
advise the Democrats on the best policies for managing capitalism’s
contradictions: we don’t share their goals! With this understanding in mind, we
prohibit “dual-carding” between our members and theirs, as we believe this is
the only way to have an honest, principled critique of the organization.

When we speak with members of the DSA, we are honest about where we stand and
our critiques of the organization. While we don’t try to actively recruit or
“poach” DSA members, we are always up front about the limits of the DSA’s
politics as we see them. While remaining tactful, we tell dissatisfied DSA
members that they should seek alternative organizations, including ours.
Anecdotally, we have received very little pushback over our criticisms from the
DSA members we encounter, and in fact many share our criticisms. Indeed,
frequently members of the DSA seem almost embarrassed by the organization and
feel the need to apologize for being members of it without prompting, saying
things like “it’s the only game in town” or “I haven’t found anything better.”

Of course, this anecdotal experience cannot be used to generalize about the
DSA’s membership as a whole, or even necessarily its organization. Our previous
conclusion that the DSA’s membership largely agrees with its politics remains.
All that can be concluded is that there are many members who see the limits of
the organization already, and are open to other politics and organizations.

Furthermore, it must be said that the DSA’s preeminence is often overstated.
While it is close to a household name, and all socialists know about it, when
actually organizing on the ground, its presence is often much smaller than its
official membership numbers would suggest. Outside of the most popular and
lowest-common-denominator rallies, marches and other events, the DSA’s
membership rarely mobilizes on the ground. The good work that is sometimes
accomplished by its members happens in spite of the leadership, not because of
it. Although we can only speculate, experience suggests the DSA’s active
membership is far smaller than its official membership rolls would suggest.

In light of these facts, we feel the best strategy is to work with the DSA in
united-front coalitions on issues we can agree on, just like any other
organization. In our coalitions, we try to expose the DSA’s leadership and the
limits of its core politics by being better organized, more determined and more
politically firm. We maintain our freedom to criticize and put forth our own
demands apart from the demands of the coalition; we do not hide our differences
with the DSA.

The feeling that one is missing out by not being part of the DSA can be
overwhelming, especially when the revolutionary left is as scattered and
disorganized as it is today. But this feeling usually leads us astray into
opportunism. Outside of very exceptional circumstances, our policy towards the
DSA must remain fundamentally the same as our policy towards any other
left-wing political organization.
